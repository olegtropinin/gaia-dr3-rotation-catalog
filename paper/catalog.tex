\title{Rotation Periods and Spin Senses of Asteroids from Gaia DR3 Sparse Photometry: Calibrated Reliability, Yarkovsky Segregation in Families and Corrections to LCDB}

\author{Oleg Tropynin}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Kazakhstan}

\begin{abstract}
I fitted a periodogram to every numbered asteroid with at least 25 Gaia DR3 transits (41{,}842 solved). The model
has season offsets, a linear phase term and a second-order Fourier series. The rotational phase is corrected for
the phase-angle bisector (PAB), with both senses of rotation tried. The fit therefore returns the spin sense as
well as the period.

Asteroids with a known period were split once into a tuning half and a test half. The period-doubling rule and a
logistic model for the probability of a correct period were fitted on the tuning half. The confidence threshold
was taken from two null tests that destroy rotation but keep the Gaia sampling.

On the test half, the 6{,}238 confident (tier A) periods are correct within 3\% in 89.3\% of cases (1303/1459),
and in 95.4\% (354/371) where the reference period has quality $U\ge3-$. Many of the remaining disagreements
are daily aliases in the ground-based reference periods. The spin sense agrees with published pole solutions
in 98\% of cases. Inside collisional families, the senses separate as the Yarkovsky drift predicts: prograde
rotators lie outside the parent's semimajor axis and retrograde ones inside it, for 74.8\% of 2{,}345 members
($p\sim10^{-132}$). The same holds for 73.6\% of the 912 members that had no published pole. I identify 108
periods in the current LCDB that are contradicted by this work and by independent data. Nine further periods
of this kind were corrected in the 2023 LCDB revision, each to the value found here. I list 861 tier-A periods with no published value in the sources checked; their expected
precision is about 90\%, and null tests limit noise to at most 3.7\%. The pipeline processes DR3 in about 20
minutes on ten cores and is meant to run on Gaia DR4 on release day.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Asteroid rotation (2211) --- Light curves (918) --- Main belt asteroids (2036) --- Photometry (1234)}

\section{Introduction}

Gaia DR3 gave sparse photometry for about 157{,}000 numbered asteroids \citep{Tanga2023}. Three groups have
derived rotation periods or spin states from these data, with different methods:
\citet{Durech2023}, \citet{Cellino2024} and \citet{MacLennan2026}. Many asteroids with enough transits still
have no published period.

Two things are needed before such periods can be used:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an estimate of how often a period is wrong;
\item the same estimate for asteroids that are \emph{not} already in other catalogues.
\end{enumerate}
This paper provides both. It also makes the pipeline fast enough to rerun on Gaia DR4, expected in December
2026 with about twice the time baseline. A pilot on 600 asteroids was reported in \citet{Tropynin2026a}.

\section{Data and geometry}

All 20 bulk files of \texttt{sso\_observation} (23.3\,M CCD rows) were averaged per \texttt{transit\_id}. This
gives 3{,}069{,}170 transits of 156{,}789 numbered asteroids. Of these, 42{,}195 asteroids have at least 25
transits. Each transit carries the largest relative flux error of its CCDs, with a 0.03\,mag floor.

Heliocentric positions come from JPL osculating elements (epoch 2026 June). These were integrated back to
2014--2017 with a fourth-order Runge--Kutta scheme, a one-day step, and the Sun and giant planets as
perturbers. Gaia's position is taken from the data, and light time is iterated. Against JPL Horizons on 19
asteroids, reduced magnitudes agree to 0.0007\,mag.

\section{Model and search}

The reduced magnitude $G-5\log_{10}(r\Delta)$ is fitted by weighted least squares. The model has three parts:
\begin{itemize}
\item one constant per observing season;
\item a term linear in phase angle;
\item a second-order Fourier series in the phase $2\pi(ft - s\,\lambda_{\rm PAB}/360^\circ)$.
\end{itemize}
Here $\lambda_{\rm PAB}$ is the ecliptic longitude of the phase-angle bisector seen from Gaia, and $s=\pm1$ is
the sense of rotation. Both senses are searched, and the better fit is kept.

Periods of 2--1000\,h are scanned on a grid with step $0.5/T$, where $T$ is the time span; the 30 deepest
minima are then refined. A grid five times finer changed precision by less than a point. If the
first-harmonic amplitude exceeds $1.20$ times the second, the doubled period is adopted, because most
lightcurves are double-peaked.

On the test set described below, the PAB correction had three effects:
\begin{itemize}
\item the fraction of correct periods rose from 45.5\% to 52.6\%;
\item the median difference from the sidereal periods of \citet{Durech2023} fell by a factor of four;
\item the period dependence of that difference, expected for an uncorrected synodic drift, largely disappeared
($9\times10^{-5}\to6\times10^{-6}$ at 2--5\,h, $3\times10^{-4}\to7\times10^{-5}$ at 10--30\,h).
\end{itemize}

\section{Calibration and null tests}

The truth set is LCDB V4.0 \citep{Warner2009} with quality $U\ge2$. Its last update (September 2021) predates
Gaia DR3. A period counts as correct if it lies within 3\% of the LCDB value. The 9{,}613 known asteroids were
split by a hash of their number into TUNE (4{,}779) and TEST (4{,}834).

On TUNE I fitted the doubling rule and a logistic model of the probability that a period is correct. The model
uses seven features: the $\chi^2$ gain over a no-rotation model, the separation from the next non-alias
minimum, the number of transits, the amplitude, the reduced $\chi^2$, the period and the harmonic ratio. On
TEST, the predicted probabilities follow the observed fractions (Figure~\ref{fig:rel}). The top bin is
slightly overconfident: 0.95 predicted against 0.89 observed.

The threshold for confident periods was not chosen on TEST. I ran two null tests on 4{,}000 random asteroids
without a known period:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{white}: the residuals of the no-rotation model were permuted within seasons;
\item \emph{block}: one-day blocks of residuals were permuted, so that correlated systematics survive.
\end{itemize}
Noise periods match no catalogue, so they all end up in the uncatalogued subset. I therefore required, before
choosing a threshold, that the projected noise be below 5\% even in that subset.

At the TUNE threshold for 80\% precision (0.384), 19.8\% (white) and 18.4\% (block) of signal-free asteroids
pass. At 0.80 the pass rates are 0.10\% and 0.05\% (Figure~\ref{fig:thr}). Tier~A is therefore defined as
probability $\ge0.80$ and period $<100$\,h. Periods of 100--1000\,h are flagged as slow candidates and are
never treated as confident.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Periods}

Of 41{,}842 solutions, 6{,}238 are in tier~A. On TEST, tier~A is correct in 1303 of 1459 cases (89.3\%): 90\%,
88\% and 83\% in the 2--10, 10--30 and 30--100\,h ranges (Figure~\ref{fig:lcdb}). The result depends strongly on
the quality of the reference period:
\begin{itemize}
\item 95.4\% (354/371) where LCDB has $U\ge3-$;
\item 89.1\% for $U=2$ and $2+$;
\item 36\% for $U=2-$.
\end{itemize}

Of the 156 disagreements:
\begin{itemize}
\item 58 differ from LCDB by exactly one cycle per day in lightcurve frequency, the daily alias of
single-site photometry;
\item 6 differ by a factor of two;
\item 92 are other periods.
\end{itemize}
Where an independent TESS period exists \citep{Vavilov2025}, it sides with this work in 20 of these 38 cases
and with LCDB in 3. Many apparent errors are therefore errors in the reference.

On TEST asteroids that appear in none of the six catalogues of Table~\ref{tab:lit}, the closest known analogue
of the unknown population, tier~A is correct in 185 of 205 cases (90\%).

The $\chi^2$-curvature errors are too small. Against \citet{Durech2023}, the median $|\Delta P/P|$ is
$3.9\times10^{-5}$. Adding $5.5\times10^{-5}P$ in quadrature gives a median $|\Delta P|/\sigma\approx0.67$. This
calibration measures agreement between two methods on the same data; it does not include aliasing.

\subsection{Injection--recovery}

I injected synthetic double-peaked lightcurves into the real Gaia sampling of the 4{,}000 null-test
asteroids. The injected lightcurves had:
\begin{itemize}
\item periods of 2--100\,h, log-uniform;
\item amplitudes of 0.1--0.6\,mag, scaled by 0.5--1 per season;
\item a random spin sense, with the phase drifting with $\lambda_{\rm PAB}$.
\end{itemize}
Two noise models bracket the real case. The pessimistic one uses the object's own permuted residuals; the
optimistic one uses Gaussian noise at the quoted errors.

In both cases tier~A is precise: 96.5\% (109/113) and 99.3\% (567/571) of its periods are correct. Tier~A
contains no $P/2$ or $2P$ errors, and the spin sense is right for 96--97\% of the correct periods.

Completeness is low: 2.7\% and 14\%, against 10.5\% of real unknown asteroids reaching tier~A. It rises with
amplitude and with the number of transits. Amplitudes below 0.2\,mag are never recovered.

Tier~A is therefore a high-precision, low-completeness selection. Without a selection-function correction it
should not be used for population statistics.

\subsection{Spin senses}

The sense $s$ chosen by the PAB fit agrees with the hemisphere of published poles in three comparisons:
\begin{itemize}
\item 3473 of 3535 asteroids (98.2\%) in \citet{Durech2023};
\item 3802 of 3899 (97.5\%) in DAMIT \citep{Durech2010};
\item 372 of 394 (94.4\%) for DAMIT asteroids that are not in \citet{Durech2023}.
\end{itemize}
Poles within $20^\circ$ of the ecliptic and ambiguous pole pairs were excluded. The catalogue gives the sense
for every tier-A asteroid: 3{,}677 retrograde and 2{,}561 prograde, of which 3{,}390 have no LCDB period.

\subsection{The Yarkovsky effect in asteroid families}

The Yarkovsky force makes prograde rotators drift outward and retrograde rotators drift inward. A
collisional family therefore spreads into a V-shape in the plane of proper semimajor axis against
$1/D$, with the two senses on opposite sides. I used family membership and synthetic proper elements from
AstDyS (June 2024) and took the namesake's proper $a$ as the centre of each family. A member is consistent
with the Yarkovsky effect if it is prograde and outside the centre, or retrograde and inside it.

Of the 2{,}345 tier-A family members, 1{,}753 (74.8\%) are consistent. The binomial probability of this
under the 50\% expected without the effect is $9\times10^{-133}$. The result does not depend on the choice
of centre: with the median $a$ of all members it is 71.3\%. It also holds for the 912 members that have no
pole in \citet{Durech2023}: 73.6\%, $p=7\times10^{-48}$. The largest families show the effect most
clearly (Figure~\ref{fig:yark}):
\begin{itemize}
\item (221) Eos: 86\% of 578;
\item (24) Themis: 81\% of 184;
\item (15) Eunomia: 79\% of 294;
\item (158) Koronis: 78\% of 184.
\end{itemize}

The spin sense is fitted from photometry alone, without information on family or semimajor axis, so this
is an independent physical check of the senses. It also adds Yarkovsky constraints for hundreds of family
members that had none.

\subsection{Errors in the reference database}

I selected tier-A asteroids whose LCDB V4.0 period disagrees with this work but where either an independent
survey supports this work and none supports LCDB \citep{Vavilov2025,McNeill2023,Erasmus2020}, or at least two
other Gaia DR3 analyses do. This gives 117 asteroids: 43 with independent support and 74 with Gaia consensus;
32 of the 117 are exact daily aliases.

In the 2023 LCDB revision distributed by the JPL Small-Body Database, 9 of the 117 have already been corrected
to the value found here. The other 108 are still present, 39 of them with independent support. The list is
part of the catalogue.

\subsection{New periods}

942 tier-A asteroids have a period neither in LCDB nor in the six catalogues. For 81 of them a rotation period
appears elsewhere:
\begin{itemize}
\item in DAMIT;
\item in about 180 VizieR tables with rotation periods (5.0\,M rows), with units taken from the table metadata
and non-rotation periods excluded;
\item in the Minor Planet Bulletin, volumes 50--53.
\end{itemize}
Where values can be compared, the disagreements have known causes:
\begin{itemize}
\item partial-coverage PTF periods that are half of ours;
\item K2 periods that differ by 3--6\%;
\item fast rotators below our 2\,h limit;
\item two slow rotators.
\end{itemize}

The remaining 861 asteroids (667 at 2--10\,h, 131 at 10--30\,h, 63 at 30--100\,h) are listed as new. The
matched control gives an expected precision of about 90\%. The null tests allow at most 16--32 noise periods,
or $\le3.7\%$ of the list. SsODNet, Behrend's Geneva page and ZTF period papers outside VizieR were not
searched.

Of the 42 candidates of \citet{Tropynin2026a}:
\begin{itemize}
\item 35 agree with this work;
\item 4 flagged ``low'' have their period doubled here;
\item 3 flagged ``high'' (39932, 109412, 13686) now prefer slow solutions outside tier~A and should be treated
as ambiguous.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and plans for Gaia DR4}

Periods below 2\,h are not searched. The $A_1/A_2$ doubling rule is a heuristic. For doubled periods the PAB
coefficient should be 2; a variant that also tries 2 improved TEST precision only from 89.3\% to 89.7\%.
Tumblers fall outside a single-period model, and completeness is low for amplitudes below about 0.3\,mag.

DR4 will have about 66 months of data. For it I plan to fit the sidereal period and the pole jointly on a
coarse pole grid, with a model-based comparison of $P$ and $2P$. Thresholds and null tests will be fixed in
advance, so that results can be released within a day.
